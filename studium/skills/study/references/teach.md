# Teaching a concept

They do not understand something and want it explained. This is the core of the job, and where
knowing the student pays most: the same question deserves a different explanation depending on
what they already hold. They have asked to be taught, so teach — generously, directly, and in
the first reply.

## Steps

1. **Locate the question in what they know.** Build the picture as SKILL.md describes: profile,
   the notes around the topic, tutor notes, the hub. Work out:
   - **the anchors** — the nearest notes they have solid, which the explanation can stand on;
   - **the chain** — what the concept depends on, and whether each link has a note and a box;
   - **the likely gap** — where the chain is weak or missing. A confusion about X is very often
     a missing piece of something X assumes;
   - **why it looked the way it did to them** — often the examples they have seen so far hide
     the distinction they are missing.

   If the course material covers the concept, read those slides so you teach the lecturer's
   version and notation.
2. **Answer, then explain.** The first line answers the question they asked ("No, not the same
   thing: one is about the state, the other about the output"). Then the explanation:
   - Start from an anchor and say so in passing: "your criterion note already has Re(λ) < 0".
   - Use their notation and, where you can, an example already in their notes.
   - Concrete before general: one specific system, mechanism or number, then the statement it
     illustrates. For this degree a real machine usually beats an abstract case.
   - Show it when the idea has a shape or moves: a computed figure, or an explorable with
     sliders when the point is how something changes. Read `visuals.md` before making one.
   - Otherwise use the form the tutor notes say lands for them: a derivation, something
     runnable. If code helps, give code they can run, and run it yourself first.
   - If two things are being confused, put them side by side and say exactly where they differ,
     and why the cases they have met so far made them look the same.
   - If a prerequisite is missing from the vault, do not stop to interrogate them about it.
     Either teach around it (derive what you need from what they have) or include the one piece
     required, and say in a clause that you did.
   - One idea, taught properly. A screen, not a chapter. Cite the slide.
3. **Check by making them use it.** End on one question that cannot be answered by repeating
   what you said: predict a changed case, explain why the earlier example hid the difference,
   say what breaks if an assumption goes. "Does that make sense?" tells you nothing. If their
   answer shows the explanation missed, take a different route, not the same one louder.
4. **Make it theirs.** Once it holds:
   - offer the stub for the concept (title, `prereqs`, `sources`, `topics`, tag `s/gap`) so the
     only thing left is for them to write it in their own words;
   - if a prerequisite turned out to be missing, propose a stub for that too; the gap will bite
     again;
   - resolve the matching entry in the hub's `## Open questions`.

   Do not `record` a grade off the back of an explanation they have just heard; that measures
   your teaching, not their memory. The first real test is the examination of the note they
   write, and the review the day after.
5. **Note what worked.** If you learned something durable about how they learn (which route
   landed, which prerequisite was silently missing), add a line to `meta/Tutor notes.md`.

## When a diagnostic question does come first

Only when you cannot choose an explanation without it: the vault points at two quite different
gaps and guessing wrong would waste the whole reply. Then ask one short question, say why, and
still give them something in that turn (the one-line answer at least).
