# Spaced review

A short daily session of recalling concepts just as they are about to fade. Practice testing
and distributed practice are the two study techniques with the strongest evidence, and this
loop is both. Fifteen to twenty minutes.

## Steps

1. **Run `status`.** If nothing is due, say so and stop there, or offer to examine an untested
   note. Reviewing early is low-value; the gap is what makes the retrieval effortful, and the
   effort is what makes it stick.
2. **Choose the set.** Up to eight notes, most overdue first, unless they give a time budget
   (allow two to three minutes each). Then reorder so that neighbouring items come from
   different courses or topics. Mixed practice is harder than blocked practice and transfers
   better, because each item starts with working out what kind of thing it is.
3. **For each note**, one at a time:
   1. Read the note and its `## Review log` yourself. Do not show it. Ask them not to open it.
   2. Pose one prompt suited to the box, and different from the last logged one:

      | Box | Prompt style |
      |---|---|
      | 1–2 | Free recall: "Explain X from scratch — what it is, why it holds, one example." |
      | 3 | Justify or perturb: derive the key step, or "what if this assumption is dropped?" |
      | 4–5 | Transfer: a small unseen problem, or a connection to a concept from another course. |

      If the log shows a past miss, aim at that weakness.
   3. Ask for a one-word prediction before they answer: `solid`, `partial` or `miss`.
   4. They answer from memory, typed or as a photo of handwriting.
   5. Grade against the note and its sources:
      - `solid` — complete and correct with no help;
      - `partial` — the core idea is right but there is a real omission or error, or they needed a cue;
      - `miss` — could not produce the core idea, or got it fundamentally wrong.

      Grade as the exam would. An error in the part they would actually use on a problem (the
      test, the condition, the formula) is a `miss` even when the surrounding words are right.
      A generous grade pushes the next review a week or a month out on something they do not
      actually have.
   6. Give specific feedback: what was missing or wrong, in a sentence or two. On a `miss`, have
      them reread their own note and explain it again straight away. It is still recorded as a
      miss, but the session should end on a successful retrieval.
   7. Record it: `record <note> <grade> --predicted <prediction> --comment "<what was missing>"`.
      Keep the comment concrete; it is what the next review will target.
   8. If their answer shows that the note itself is wrong or incomplete, say so and add a
      question to the note's `## Open questions`. They fix the note; you do not.
4. **Close.** A small table of results, one sentence on calibration, and the next due date.
   Calibration is the comparison between predictions and outcomes: "you predicted solid five
   times and were solid twice" is more useful to them than any single grade, because
   overconfidence is what makes people stop studying too early.

## When the answer arrives with the request

If they send an answer together with the note name ("here is my recall for Subspaces,
predicted solid: …"), skip the prompting: grade it, give feedback, and record it.
